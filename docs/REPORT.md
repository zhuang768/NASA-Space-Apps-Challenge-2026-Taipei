## Abstract

We wanted to find out which observations would make a useful first test of shallow ground ice in three northern regions of Mars. We screened regional topography, thermal inertia, ice consistency, dust and temperature data, then returned to the original grids to check three fixed regions. The screen examined 172,800 nominal cells. It retained 58,238 for comparison and produced a set of 453 candidates for which no other candidate was at least as good on every criterion and better on at least one. We reviewed 22 examples and retained MS-01, MS-02 and MS-03 for follow-up.

The Earth part asks what we could learn from a specific site and measurement, rather than trying to find a place that resembles Mars in every way. A cold desert pair can help test a thermal interpretation. An ice wedge with core observations can help design a radar survey. A high-resolution polygon map can show what becomes difficult to measure when image resolution drops. At this stage these are useful test cases. The data do not establish three new validated Earth analog pairs, the presence of ice in the three Mars boxes, or a safe place to land.

## 1. Research question

For each Mars region, what observation would reduce the most important uncertainty, and is there an Earth site where that observation can be tested against independent evidence?

A useful analog depends on the question. A site with good drilling records may be a strong test for radar and a poor match for surface temperature. An Earth site with similar looking polygons may help test a mapping method, even when the polygons formed under different conditions. We therefore treated each Mars and Earth pairing as a separate, testable proposal. We recorded the data already available, what had been checked before, and what would make us change our interpretation.

The project addresses the Mars side of the [NASA Space Apps challenge on new terrestrial analogs](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/2026/challenges/permanent-moon-base-locations-and-mars/). It does not rank lunar sites or validate future bases. We used four themes to keep comparisons consistent: terrain, temperature and thermal properties, water and ice, and the atmosphere or dust. Some Earth and Mars measurements do not have a comparable quantity or spatial scale, so we kept those differences visible instead of combining them in one score.

## 2. Mars data and screening

We used [MOLA elevation](https://doi.org/10.17189/1519460) to summarize relief and slope. Relief is the difference between the highest and lowest sampled elevations in a region. The slope calculation represents a kilometer-scale neighborhood, about 3.7 km in the native data, so it does not describe individual rocks or short slopes where a rover might get stuck. We used the [TES nighttime thermal inertia product](https://pds-geosciences.wustl.edu/missions/mgs/tes-timap.html), with its interpolation mask, to compare how the surface responds to heating and cooling. The TES data do not tell us directly whether the material is hard, wet or ice-rich.

The [SWIM 2.0 source products](https://swim.psi.edu/SWIM2Products.php) combine evidence for or against ice in the 0 to 1 m and 1 to 5 m depth bands. Here those numbers are depth ranges. The consistency index itself is signed. A score can be high without measuring the amount of ice, and the two bands can draw on overlapping evidence. We treated positive scores as a way to narrow the search and did not count agreement between the bands as two independent ground observations.

The [Montabone dust scenarios](https://doi.org/10.1029/2019JE006111) provided reconstructed column dust optical depth for Mars Years 34 and 35. We used each year's 95th percentile to compare relatively dusty conditions without ranking the sites on one maximum. This is a regional column estimate, not a local record of wind, visibility near the ground or dust deposited on equipment. The [time-adjusted MCS temperatures](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17947556) provided broad temperature context using paired 03:00 and 15:00 local-time bins. The native support is coarse, about 5 degrees, and many bins are missing. We did not treat these values as continuous, local weather records.

The screen covered nominal half-degree grid cells from 59.75 degrees south to 59.75 degrees north. Of 172,800 cells, 172,581 had finite values for the main variables. Both SWIM indicators were positive in 59,332 cells. After the documented historical exclusions, 58,238 remained in the comparison pool. The exclusions served to separate this review from the project's earlier targets. They do not establish that the retained regions had never been studied.

We used six criteria: higher shallow and deeper SWIM scores, lower slope and relief, and lower dust percentiles in both years. A cell stays on the Pareto front if no other cell is at least as good on every criterion and better on at least one. This avoids having to decide how much improvement in dust should compensate for a larger slope. The front contained 453 cells. We drew 22 geographically spread examples for review. The detailed native-grid check covered four targets, A, B, C and D. A, D and C became MS-01, MS-02 and MS-03. B remained an exploratory comparison near a published ice exposure. The final boxes are half a degree by half a degree, with north latitude and east longitude reported here.

## 3. The three Mars regions

### MS-01, 56.75 N, 72.75 E

MS-01 has a mean regional slope of 0.287 degrees and 76 m of relief. The TES mean is 325.2 J per square meter per kelvin per square root second, based on 73 valid native cells out of 100. The shallow and deeper SWIM means are 0.639 and 0.647. This site raises a thermal question: how much of the temperature behavior could come from the surface material and cover rather than ice below it? The 0.427 m depth from the published two-layer model is relevant as a test scale. It is not a measured interface.

### MS-02, 52.75 N, 168.25 E

MS-02 is the southernmost of the three retained regions. It has 117 m of relief, a mean slope of 0.302 degrees and a TES mean of 217.3 in the same units. It has the largest valid TES fraction, 93 out of 100 cells. The shallow and deeper SWIM means are 0.693 and 0.622. The MY34 dust 95th percentile is 0.240, the highest of the three. In order from MS-01 to MS-03, the MY34 values are 0.222, 0.240 and 0.173; the MY35 values are 0.180, 0.173 and 0.141. The conditional interface depth is 0.450 m. The question here is whether a local survey could distinguish a shallow interface from other reflectors. A regional dust value and an orbital radar return cannot make that distinction by themselves.

### MS-03, 57.75 N, 242.25 E

MS-03, also written as 117.75 W, has 76 m of relief and the lowest mean slope, 0.163 degrees. Its mean TES thermal inertia is 152.8, based on 79 valid cells out of 100. The shallow SWIM mean of 0.911 is the highest of the three, while its deeper mean is 0.510. Its conditional model depth is 0.103 m. We used the contrast between the shallow and deeper indicators to frame a question about surface cover and image resolution. We did not infer a thin, confirmed ice layer from those values.

The conditional depths above come from the [Piqueux et al. seasonal surface temperature model](https://doi.org/10.1029/2019GL083947). Its two-layer interpretation assumes a frozen lower material. Applying that model to a regional cell does not supply drilling data or a site-level error bar. The analysis retained 69, 75 and 60 paired MCS bins out of 288 nominal bins at MS-01, MS-02 and MS-03, respectively. We used those counts to describe the temperature evidence without treating empty bins as zero or as new observations.

## 4. Testing the Mars questions on Earth

We chose the Earth sites according to the question each one could help answer. A site with temperature sensors and exposed ground ice can help test a thermal interpretation. A site with radar and boreholes can help test what produces a reflector. A place with mapped polygons can help test whether an image has enough detail to show them. These are separate experiments. A close match on one measure does not establish a match in climate, geology or ice content.

We also looked for prior work at each named field site and for experiments that had already used it in the same way. Most of our useful references were known field sites. We describe the experiment that would make each comparison useful and state whether we ran it, derived a simpler test, or could only outline it with the data available.

### MS-01: Thermal evidence and shallow ground ice

At MS-01, the published Mars thermal model gives an interface depth of 0.427 m. The model estimates the depth from seasonal surface temperatures under an assumption about the material in the lower layer. No local observation independently confirms the depth or composition of that interface ([Piqueux et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1029/2019GL083947)).

Table Mountain in Antarctica provides a physical reference. At two stations, thermistors were installed alongside core observations. The ice-cemented boundary was measured at about 0.45 m at TM1 and 0.14 m at TM2. The sensor spacing was 0.135 m. The 0.023 m difference between TM1 and the modeled MS-01 depth is smaller than that spacing, so we do not use it to score the analogy. Both Earth stations contained ice, and their materials differed. The site was already studied in a Mars context. Its value here is as a reference for a future blinded thermal test, not as a new analog site ([Pringle et al., 2003](https://doi.org/10.1029/2002JB002364)).

The NGEE Arctic record from Barrow, Alaska, illustrates why a depth and a temperature pattern can be misleading on their own. We reanalyzed the 2013 to 2014 record from two sensors in the same soil pit. At 0.42 m, 138 consecutive hourly readings were within 0.2 degrees C of 0 degrees C while the sensor at 0.15 m was below -1 degree C. The first and last timestamps were 137 hours apart. The documentation gives conflicting time-zone labels, so the calculation used the printed time intervals from a single logger. Seasonal freezing could produce a pattern like this. We cannot identify the cause from this record alone, and no independent core at the same location establishes the presence or absence of permanent ice at that depth. [NGEE Arctic soil observations](https://doi.org/10.5440/1126515); [prior thermal-hydrology study at Barrow](https://doi.org/10.5194/gmd-13-2259-2020).

Edson Hills in West Antarctica is a candidate for testing how well a thermal method separates dry frozen soil from ice-cemented ground. EL18 and EL19 are about 155 m apart; the field descriptions report ice-cemented ground at 15 cm and dry permafrost at 25 cm, respectively. EL21 and EL20 are about 144 m apart, with the corresponding contacts reported at 30 cm and 35 cm. The two publications use the same sample labels, coordinates and horizon temperatures, so we treated them as one set of field observations. The ice-cemented points are on valley floors, whereas the dry points are on slopes. The studies report temperatures taken at individual soil horizons, but we did not find a continuous ground-temperature record at those four pits. At 90 to 100 m native sampling, satellite thermal observations are too coarse to separate ice effects from differences in slope, shade and surface material in these particular pairs. [Delpupo et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geomorph.2017.07.002); [Heritage Range soil study, 2025](https://www.scielo.br/j/aabc/a/KvTyQ9HRN79XssVGzWPdwWM/?lang=en); [NASA ASTER surface-temperature product](https://doi.org/10.5067/ASTER/AST_08.004).

The next useful experiment at Edson Hills would record surface and subsurface temperatures at matching depths on comparable terrain, with independent field descriptions of both dry and ice-cemented material. The interpretation should be tested against those descriptions only after the temperature-based classification is fixed.

### MS-02: Separating buried ice from other radar reflectors

The Bethel field survey in Alaska gives us a way to ask whether ground radar can distinguish a buried ice wedge from the boundary of a thawed layer. The published drilling records contain three adjacent intersections with an ice wedge. The tops of the wedge were at 0.78 to 0.89 m in those holes. Five surrounding holes did not record a wedge in the sampled profiles, although that does not make those profiles ice-free. Jones and colleagues describe near-surface permafrost in the inland Yukon-Kuskokwim Delta as relatively poorly documented compared with other Arctic and sub-Arctic settings. That is a regional statement. The Bethel radar and drilling survey is already in the literature. Its wet peat also makes it a useful Earth stress test, not an assumed description of MS-02. [Jones et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/ppp.70004); [Bethel field data](https://doi.org/10.18739/A24B2X68B).

We checked fourteen files from the archive, including raw 160 MHz radar records, GPS traces and borehole documentation. The likely radar file, DAT_0408, has 210 traces and a nominal trace interval of about 0.50 m. The field map draws the drilling transect separately from the line used for radar and manual thaw probing. Distance measured along one line cannot be used as position on the other. The radar file also reports October 2, 2024, whereas the BET-31 probing record reports October 1. We found no public survey crosswalk that fixes both the locations and the timing. The published radar thaw-depth interpretation used manual probes for calibration, so comparing it to those same probes would not be an independent test. We did not calculate a radar detection rate. [Bethel study and maps](https://doi.org/10.1002/ppp.70004).

A simplified calculation still shows why spacing matters. If the adjacent confirmed intersections belong to one continuous wedge interval bounded by the nearest holes without a wedge, its projected width on the drilling transect would be about 0.85 to 1.25 m. Under that assumption, equally spaced point samples with a random starting offset would hit the interval in roughly 43 to 63 percent of cases at 2 m spacing. This is a geometric calculation without a radar signal model. It does not test echoes, attenuation, clutter or the continuity of ice at a single depth.

Eight Mile Lake offers a separate test for false ice identifications. Seven of nine georeferenced core records include a year-round unfrozen layer, called a talik, with tops at 13 to 67 cm. The authors report about 17 cm root mean square error for radar estimates of the top of the unfrozen layer. We checked the archived core table but did not independently align each core with a radar trace. These records can help identify a competing explanation for a reflector. Bethel and Eight Mile Lake would be useful for evaluating a future surface radar workflow; they do not validate a sub-meter ice interface from existing orbital radar at MS-02. [Eight Mile Lake data](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17176584); [Eight Mile Lake preprint](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2025/egusphere-2025-4667/).

### MS-03: How image resolution changes what we can map

For MS-03, the immediate question is whether the fixed box, 57.50 to 58.00°N and 242.00 to 242.50°E, contains imagery that supports complete polygon outlines. The [repository-only audit](../outputs/research/MS-03/repository_validation_20261115/README.md) used the existing five CTX display windows, three fixed MOC windows and one supplemental native CTX crop. No set passed the required eight-sample narrow-width gate, three repeatable bends/intersections and two adjacent complete closed units. Polygon width, area and density remain NA. This is an evidence limit, not proof that polygons do not exist.

The retained MOC masked image has 9,272,742 nonzero in-box sample centres. On its stated spherical projection, this represents approximately 71.1407 km² or 15.178% of the box. The three MOC windows actually inspected cover 6.0334 km² or 1.287%; the five CTX display tiles cover 9.6611 km² or 2.061%. These supports overlap and are not added. Nonzero pixels and catalogue-corner containment are separate from image quality and accepted contour area. MOC's 3.000339463 m projected grid is not an independently established optical resolution; the full original MOC IMG is not retained. The D01 CTX native byte subset is present and its displayed crop reproduces exactly, but its uncalibrated DN and approximate camera location do not support precise ground-outline area.

AD1 in Adventdalen is a known geometry/method reference. The actual archived shapefile contains 185 closed source polygons. Recalculated areas, perimeters, equivalent diameters and circularities match the retained table. This validates descriptive readback, not independent outline accuracy. Its median minimum Feret width is 16.3403 m. Under a size-only necessary bound, 184 of 185 source shapes fall below eight nominal CTX 6 m samples, and 152 fall below eight MOC local-ground samples. The remaining shapes are only not excluded by size; none is a validated Mars detection. No Earth raster, independent second flight or blind outline set was available for a real image-degradation or IoU test.

A later main integration supplies an executable AD1 grid-phase test on the same published vectors: 185 shapes × four spacings × 16 deterministic offsets. It measures digital geometry preservation, not recognition from an image or independent contour accuracy. The replay and its scope are linked in the [later integration record](CHALLENGE_ALIGNMENT.md#later-main-integration). It does not supply accepted MS-03 Mars boundaries.

Cape Blossom remains a proposed follow-up source. The previous draft claimed 1,927 centres and 400 grid offsets, and a separate Samoylov test with numeric IoU results. The audit did not find those source centres, closed labels, georeferenced imagery, scripts or result tables across the searched GitHub records. Those computations are therefore not reported as completed evidence. Their external DOI citations are retained in the [correction record](CHALLENGE_ALIGNMENT.md#claims-corrected-in-this-update), without implying that a link supplies the underlying data. AD1 is not a newly discovered replacement site, and its prior Earth/Mars geomorphometry is recorded.

A stronger comparison needs qualified in-box Mars boundaries and Earth image/outline truth at a common physical scale, with documented sampling, registration and uncertainty. Shape resemblance alone cannot establish ice or a common formation mechanism.

### Status of the analog comparisons

Each comparison establishes a narrower next test: thermal ambiguity for MS-01, radar ambiguity for MS-02, and image sampling for MS-03. Edson Hills, Bethel and Cape Blossom are narrative follow-up proposals for those separate purposes; their missing repository source packages must be delivered before their calculations can be claimed as independently reproducible. The open evidence did not establish a validated Earth match for any of the three Mars sites. We also did not establish that any of the candidate Earth sites or their uses were new. Our results support doing more focused tests and show what the available observations can and cannot distinguish.
## 5. What the results establish

The three Mars sites survived a documented screen and a second check at the resolution of the source products. They serve different purposes. At MS-01 the main question is how to tell a material effect from a change at an ice interface. At MS-02 the useful test is whether a future surface radar can distinguish ice from a thaw boundary or another reflector. At MS-03 the first question is whether the available imagery can resolve the shape that we want to compare.

The three limitations are different enough that a single similarity score would be misleading. The Edson Hills field observations identify contrasting materials, but terrain is confounded with the ice category. The Bethel cores identify actual ice wedges, but individual raw radar traces cannot yet be assigned to those cores. Cape Blossom is a proposed geometry source whose input package is not retained here. A centre-point dataset would not provide closed-outline truth or measurements of ice beneath each shape. None of these gaps disappears by finding another numerical value close to a Mars model estimate.

We checked both site history and method history. For example, Table Mountain was already studied in a Mars context and the Bethel archive was originally collected for Earth permafrost research. A new calculation on known data can be useful even when the underlying site is not new. Our search of published work, data archives and conference material was bounded. We do not use a missing search result to claim that a site is unstudied, that the work is a first, or that it qualifies for an award.

## 6. Verification and reproduction

The repository keeps the fixed target coordinates, numerical tables, source information and rebuild scripts. The input manifest identifies 11 public Mars files, totaling 383,526,092 bytes. It records download addresses, expected file sizes and SHA-256 checksums. The workflow checks those bytes before using them. We used Python 3.12 on an Apple Silicon Mac and did not need a graphics processor or a trained model for the Mars screen.

A clean rebuild reproduced the membership of the comparison pool and the Pareto front, the order of the 22 review examples, and all 88 compared numerical columns in the native-grid output. The dust series and MCS bin files matched byte for byte. The complete rebuilt NetCDF stack did not have the same checksum as the original. The rebuild record shows small floating-point differences for some global arrays. They did not change the selected coordinates or the native summaries in this check. We report both facts because matching the selected results does not mean that every input-derived file is identical.

The numerical validation also checked coordinate uniqueness, valid source coverage, the TES interpolation exclusion, 669 reconstructed dust sols per year for each detailed-review target and consistent matching of the MCS time bins. Those checks test the recorded computation. They do not validate the assumptions of the thermal model or demonstrate the presence of ground ice. The retained Earth records and the newer narrative proposals have different reproducibility status. This update reproduced 70 MS-03 numeric fields from exact GitHub archival inputs, inspected the fixed image windows and remeasured AD1 vectors. It did not independently rerun the newer Edson/Bethel/Cape source packages or the full global rebuild. Historical global validation results above remain attributed to their saved records. Proposed Earth calculations require their actual source bytes, code and outputs before being counted as completed tests. For example, dividing a polygon width by the pixel pitch is a geometry check. Recovering a boundary from a real image requires a separate test.

Use the numerical-only path in the [rebuild instructions](https://github.com/zhuang768/NASA-Space-Apps-Challenge-2026-Taipei/blob/main/docs/REPRODUCE.md) in a fresh working copy. The rebuild replaces local generated outputs. Use the `--skip-report` option so it does not recreate historical report files. The [Mars source manifest](https://github.com/zhuang768/NASA-Space-Apps-Challenge-2026-Taipei/blob/main/config/raw_inputs_manifest.json) and the [data attribution record](https://github.com/zhuang768/NASA-Space-Apps-Challenge-2026-Taipei/blob/main/DATA_LICENSES.md) identify product versions and reuse conditions. Repository links require access to the team repository. Primary data and papers are cited separately above.

## 7. Next tests

At Edson Hills we would first look for ground-temperature profiles with documented positions and sensor depths. If those do not exist for the same soil units, the useful field design would compare dry frozen and ice-cemented ground on similar terrain with the same instruments and sampling schedule. A test of an ice-depth estimator would hold back independent subsurface observations until after the estimate was fixed.

At Bethel the first deliverable is a registration check, not a detection statistic. The drilling line and the radar line need a common surveyed reference, the correct orientation and matched timing. Once those are confirmed, a separate line or spatial block should be held out from calibration. The survey should record missed targets as well as detections. The Eight Mile Lake data can then be used as a separate control for a reflector caused by an unfrozen layer.

At Cape Blossom we would take actual high-resolution imagery and independently mapped outlines and compare them under several controlled degradations. We would preserve the original labels, vary the sampling and contrast, and compare the resulting maps without giving the interpreter the original boundaries. The measured output would be the proportion of known polygons recovered and the overlap of their outlines. Before applying that test to MS-03, we would verify exactly which Mars images cover the box and what their effective resolution and noise allow.

## Conclusion

TerraMatch turns a broad search for shallow ground ice into three questions that can be tested separately. The Mars screening and selected regional summaries can be reproduced. The retained method references and the source-status audit identify where depth matching, radar registration and image sampling could lead to stronger conclusions than the available evidence supports. The remaining work is to test the measurements that connect each Earth example to the corresponding Mars question. Until then, the three boxes are research targets, not confirmed ice deposits or recommended landing sites.

The observations in this report come from the cited investigators and archives. AI assistance was used in code and data checks and in preparing this draft; it did not supply field observations or ground truth.
